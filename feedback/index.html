    Project: Can machine learning improve the targeting of alcohol-prevention programmes in Germany? (ESS Round 11, 2,403 respondents, target = frequent binge drinking.)

    88/ 100
    Criterion Weight Points In one line
    Framing: question, target, decision, cost of each mistake 20 17 Clear decision and cost ratio. The "fixed budget of 100" plan and the threshold rule are two different decisions.
    Data preparation, documented (incl. missing values) 20 18 A codebook-based missing-value table, the best in the group. Survey weights and the "frequent" cut-off need one line each.
    Pipeline and validation: split, baseline, CV, no leakage 30 26 Threshold chosen on out-of-fold predictions, exactly right. The test set was used more than once (−3).
    Metric and threshold, read honestly 20 18 The most honest reading in the group. Make "target everyone" the baseline your model has to beat.
    Clarity of the memo and the assistant log 10 9 Clear prose and a log with real rejections.

    Cell numbers count from the top of your notebook, starting at 1.

    What already works

    • The decision is real and the costs are explicit. A government chooses whom to contact, you name both errors (wasted outreach and stigma vs. missed prevention), and you fix FN = 3 × FP before modelling. You also say clearly that the model is not causal (cell 1).
    • The target is built with care. Abstainers are coded 0 from alcfreq, not dropped, and only the 17 true non-responses are removed, with the reason given (cells 9–10, 16).
    • Missing values are explained from the codebook. Refusals, "don't know" and not-applicable are told apart. A separate missing category for income (246 rows, 10%) is the right call, because refusing to report income is itself informative (cell 16).
    • The threshold is chosen correctly. You chose it from cross_val_predict on the training data (cells 32–33), which is the method the course asks for.
    • You admitted the test-set slip and limited what you claim because of it (cells 34 and 45). That is exactly the professional habit we want.

    Criterion by criterion

    Framing · 17/20
    • Pick one decision rule. Cell 1 promises a fixed budget of 100 people judged by recall. With a fixed budget the threshold no longer matters: you rank everyone by predicted risk and contact the top 100. The right metrics are then precision@100 and recall@100, and the threshold analysis answers a different question ("whom would we contact if the budget were free?"). Both are legitimate. Say which one the ministry faces, or show both and explain when each applies.
    • Back the 3:1 ratio with numbers. Sacks et al. (2015) estimate the total cost of excessive drinking. That is not the cost of missing one person relative to contacting one person. A back-of-the-envelope version is enough: outreach per person ≈ €X; a missed case costs (treatment cost avoided) × (probability that prevention works) ≈ €Y; ratio ≈ Y/X. Then the 3 is an assumption a reader can disagree with, instead of a number that comes from nowhere.
    Data preparation · 18/20
    • Justify "monthly or more" as frequent. It is a defensible choice, close to the WHO definition of heavy episodic drinking (at least monthly). Say so in one line with the source, because the target definition drives everything else.
    • Survey weights. The 29.9% prevalence is unweighted. That is fine for training a classifier, but say it explicitly, and use the ESS weights (anweight) whenever you state a share of German adults.
    • sclmeet is in your extract but never discussed. Social-meeting frequency could well predict binge drinking. Would a ministry have it at prediction time? Probably not, and that is the reason to exclude it. Write that reason down, as you did for the alcohol variables.
    Pipeline and validation · 26/30
    • Test set used more than once (−3). Cell 30 scores the model on the test set at 0.5 before the threshold is chosen, and the extra tuning you describe in cell 34 also touched it. See One rule for everyone below.
    • The threshold grid is coarse. You tried six values. Use something like np.linspace(0.05, 0.6, 56). Also add a sanity check: with FN = 3 and FP = 1 the cost-minimising threshold for calibrated probabilities is 1 / (1 + 3) = 0.25. Your cross-validation found exactly 0.25. Say that, because it shows theory and data agree.
    • The KNN search is well done. The grid goes wide enough (1 to 500) to show the curve flatten, and you read k = 200 correctly as a sign that the features carry little local structure.
    Metric and threshold · 18/20
    • Make your best finding the headline. Cell 45 notes that targeting everyone costs 421 against your model's 403. That is the key result. When the prevalence (30%) is above the cost threshold (25%), the cheapest trivial policy is not "contact nobody", it is "contact everyone". That is the baseline your model has to beat. On your own training out-of-fold numbers, "everyone" costs 1,263 (all 1,263 non-drinkers contacted) and your best rule costs 1,158, about 8% cheaper. On the test set the saving is about 4%. Report it that way.
    • Call the model comparison a tie. The AUCs are 0.605 vs. 0.598 (cell 42), and the KNN AUC varies by about ±0.02 across folds (cell 36). That difference is within noise. "Logistic regression is preferred because it is simpler and equally good" is the honest version.
    • Narrow the conclusion. "Socioeconomic and demographic characteristics cannot reliably predict binge drinking" (cell 43) should say these eight variables. Other observable variables might do better.
    Clarity and assistant log · 9/10
    • The log has real rejections and corrections, including the test-set slip. Keep that standard.
    • Fix one unclear row: "I initially kept preprocessing inside the pipeline". Did you keep it there, or move it later?

    Priorities for the final delivery (due Thu 26 Nov)

    1. Freeze the test set and redo every comparison on out-of-fold predictions (see below). This feeds the final criterion Response to feedback and quality of the final pipeline.
    2. Settle the decision rule. Your three strategies are an excellent design: random, demographic-only (age + gender) and full model, with 100 contacts each. Evaluate them with precision@100 / recall@100 on out-of-fold predictions. Random gives about 30 hits per 100; how many does each model give?
    3. Three families of methods:
      • your logistic regression;
      • a random forest or gradient boosting (Session 6), which can pick up interactions such as age × gender × activity that a logit misses;
      • LASSO (Session 7), which answers "do we need all eight variables, or are age and gender enough?". That is exactly your strategy 2 vs. strategy 3 question.
    4. Quantify the recommendation. For example: "per 1,000 contacts the model reaches N frequent binge drinkers vs. 300 at random, saving €Z under assumptions A, B, C".
    5. Limitations. Drinking is self-reported, there is a gap between survey and register data, and income and employment may not be available to the ministry.

    Criterion by criterion

    Framing · 16/20

    • Who exactly is worth a call? Target = 1 means the household already has ISAPRE. In your confusion matrix at 0.143 (cell 26):

      • The 244 true positives are households that are already privately insured, possibly by this ISAPRE.
      • The 399 false positives are FONASA households that look like ISAPRE clients. Under the story in cell 5 ("call those whose profile looks like that of its current clients"), they are the leads.

      So in your decision, the "mistake" is the product. Choose one of two stories and rewrite the costs to match:

      • (a) Poaching from competing ISAPREs: true positives are the prospects. Then you need to know which ISAPRE they use, or assume a share.
      • (b) A look-alike model to convert FONASA households: predicted positives among FONASA households are the leads. The value of a call is then (conversion rate) × (margin of a client), and "false positive" no longer means "wasted call".

      This is the single most important fix for the final delivery.

    • Argue the 6:1 ratio. "Losing a client costs six calls" needs a reason. A rough figure is enough: the cost of a call in CLP and the expected margin of a new client over a year.

    Data preparation · 18/20
    • The groups you dropped will still be on the call list. You removed 708 households (armed forces, none, other, missing) (cell 9). That is fine for defining the target, but a real call list will include them. Say how the model would treat them, or why they can be excluded before calling.
    • Survey weights. The 10.8% ISAPRE share is unweighted. CASEN's survey_weight turns it into a population share. Mention it whenever you talk about "households in Chile".
    • Overlapping variables. head_activity and the new head_job_type = not_working carry overlapping information. That is harmless for the logit, but worth a line, and it matters for LASSO in Session 7.

    • Check the theoretical threshold with cross-validation. 1/(1 + c) is the optimal threshold only if the predicted probabilities are calibrated. Check it on out-of-fold predictions: cross_val_predict(final_model, X_train, y_train, cv=cv, method="predict_proba"), then the cost for each threshold on a fine grid. A quick reproduction of your pipeline gives a CV-optimal threshold of about 0.14, which agrees with the formula. Put that confirmation in the notebook.

    • Notebook hygiene.

      • The execution counts skip 12, 15, 17 and 21, so cells were re-run out of order.

    One rule for everyone: the test set

    All three Checkpoint 1 notebooks used the test set more than once. That happens to almost everyone the first time, and it is easy to fix. The same deduction (−3 in Pipeline and validation) applies to all three projects, so being honest about it cost you nothing extra.

    For the final delivery:

    1. Define one cv = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=42) near the top and pass that same object to every GridSearchCV, cross_val_score and cross_val_predict. The final rubric asks for a comparison "with cross-validation on the same splits", and this is how you get it.
    2. Do every comparison on out-of-fold predictions from the training set. That covers model vs. model, threshold vs. threshold and cost ratio vs. cost ratio. cross_val_predict(..., method="predict_proba") gives you one honest probability per training row, and you can compute any confusion matrix, cost or curve from it.
    3. Touch the test set once, at the very end, for the model and threshold you have already chosen. Print that single result and do not change anything after seeing it.
    4. Say it in the report: your test set was already seen in Checkpoint 1, so the final test number is slightly optimistic. One sentence is enough. It is a strength, not a weakness.

    Before you submit, run Runtime → Restart and run all and check that every number in your text matches a printed output.
