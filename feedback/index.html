    Fix before you submit

    1. Your model choice contradicts your own reading of the results

    Cell 51 says the differences between models are "almost negligible and may reflect sampling variation", and then picks KNN because it found 44 hits against LASSO's 43 and the logit's 42. Both statements cannot be true at once. A quick sense of the noise: with 100 contacts and a hit rate around 42%, the standard error is √(0.42 × 0.58 / 100) ≈ 0.05. That is about ±10 hits for a 95% interval. A 2-hit gap is well inside it.

    What to do:

    • Show the noise. Compute precision@k inside each fold (the top 20 of each of the 5 folds) and report the mean and standard deviation per model. Alternatively, bootstrap the out-of-fold predictions about 1,000 times. Either way, put an interval next to each number in the comparison table (cell 49).
    • Then choose on the tie-breakers that matter to a ministry: interpretability, the data it needs and stability. That points to the logistic regression, or even the demographic-only model, not KNN. KNN also has the lowest AUC of the full models (0.598) and cannot explain why someone was selected. That is awkward for a programme that must avoid stigma, a point you raise yourself in cell 54.
    • Check KNN for ties. With k = 200, KNN probabilities can only take values in steps of 1/200 = 0.005, so many people near the top-100 cut-off share the same score, and argsort breaks those ties arbitrarily. Run np.sort(p_knn)[-110:] to see how many tied values sit around position 100. If many are tied, the "44" partly depends on row order.
    • Note the metric mismatch. k was chosen by AUC (cell 33) but the model is judged by precision@100. One sentence is enough; optionally, choose k by precision@100.
    2. The "per 1,000 contacts" extrapolation (cell 54) needs its condition

    "KNN would identify approximately 440 frequent binge drinkers per 1,000 contacts" holds only if those 1,000 contacts are still the top 5.5% of the population, the same share as 100 out of 1,802 in your training set. That would mean screening about 18,000 people. If the ministry contacts more people from the same population, precision falls, because you go further down the ranking. Your test evaluation already gets this right: 33 contacts out of 601 keeps the same 5.5% share (cell 53).

    What to do: state the budget as a share ("we contact the top 5.5% of screened adults"), and show precision at a few shares (5%, 10%, 20%) on the out-of-fold predictions. A small table or a curve of precision vs. share contacted is enough. That tells the ministry what happens if the budget grows.

    3. Quantify the recommendation

    The final rubric asks for the expected effect with explicit assumptions. Right now the recommendation stops at "hits per 100 contacts". Add one back-of-the-envelope paragraph:

    Per 1,000 contacts at the top 5.5%: N_model frequent binge drinkers reached vs. about 300 at random → (N_model − 300) extra people reached. If outreach costs €X per person and prevention avoids €Y of later costs per frequent drinker reached, with effectiveness e, the net gain is (N_model − 300) × e × Y − 1,000 × X.

    Use explicit placeholders with a source for each one, even rough ones (your Sacks et al. reference can anchor Y). Then compare the full model with the demographic-only model in the same units. "Collecting six more variables buys about 50 extra people per 1,000" (cell 54) is the right question; give it a euro value, or the break-even cost of collecting those variables.

    4. Tune the random forest (cell 42)

    The forest uses fixed settings (min_samples_leaf=5, n_estimators=300). Session 6 tunes the tree-size parameters with cross-validation. A small GridSearchCV over min_samples_leaf ∈ {5, 20, 50} and max_depth ∈ {None, 5, 10} with your cv takes minutes and closes the "was the forest given a fair chance?" question. A forest with leaves of 5 on 1,800 noisy survey rows is likely to overfit. Larger leaves will probably help.

    5. Show the LASSO path, not only the CV optimum (cells 45–47)

    At the CV-best C = 1.0 the penalty is weak, so it is not surprising that every original variable survives. Your question was can the ministry collect fewer variables? To answer it, run the path as in Session 7: for C in np.logspace(-3, 0, 8), record the CV precision@100 (or AUC) and how many of the 8 original variables keep at least one non-zero coefficient. Then you can say "with 4 variables we keep X% of the performance", which links directly to your demographic-only comparison.

    6. Give age a fair shape in the demographic-only model (cell 39)

    Age enters the logit as a straight line, but binge drinking is typically highest among young adults and falls with age. That pattern is not linear in the log-odds. Use age bands (for example 18–29, 30–44, 45–64, 65+) or add age². If the demographic-only model improves, your conclusion "age and gender get you most of the way" gets stronger.

    7. Interpret what drives the risk

    Use the LASSO or logistic coefficients (cell 46) for a short paragraph on who is flagged. For example: living on a farm or in the countryside raises the risk, being female lowers it, and so does being widowed. Say that these are associations, not causes. This answers the "interpret" part of the brief and helps you defend the targeting rule ethically.

    8. Limitations: what would break in production

    Your limitations paragraph (cell 54) covers self-reporting and generalisation. Add:

    • the ministry does not hold survey answers, so which administrative sources would supply age, gender, income and marital status;
    • the ESS fieldwork is from 2023–24 and behaviour shifts;
    • the ethical constraint: a model flagging people by gender, marital status or income for "alcohol risk" needs a careful outreach design. You mention it; make it a concrete design rule.

    • Remove the leftover notes in cells 22 and 29 ("Because of the pre in the cell above…"). They refer to an earlier version of the notebook.
    • Cell 54: the sentence with parentheses "(precision@33) … (test set) … 44 (13)" is hard to read. Split it into one sentence for the CV result and one for the test result.
    • "may be a reasonable alternatives" → "alternative".
    • Report the random baseline's recall@100 the same way in cell 24 and cell 49 (the two cells compute it with different formulas, even though the values agree).

    Framing · 17/20
    • Pick one decision rule. Cell 1 promises a fixed budget of 100 people judged by recall. With a fixed budget the threshold no longer matters: you rank everyone by predicted risk and contact the top 100. The right metrics are then precision@100 and recall@100, and the threshold analysis answers a different question ("whom would we contact if the budget were free?"). Both are legitimate.
