    Session 8 · Unlabeled Data: PCA and Clustering¶

    So far every model had an answer to learn from: income (Sessions 3–4) or the label poor (Sessions 5–7). That is supervised learning.

    Today's question. Programmes are designed for profiles, such as older people living alone, young families with children or overcrowded households. They are not designed for a single score. What types of households are there, if we never tell the algorithm who is poor? This is unsupervised learning: there is no target, only the features.

    What is new today: PCA (principal components, explained variance, loadings, scores), k-means (inertia, the elbow, the silhouette), hierarchical clustering with a dendrogram, and cluster profiling.

    The rule for today: income and poor are never inputs. We look at poverty only after the clusters exist, to describe them.

    1. Choose what describes a household¶

    Clustering finds groups in the variables you give it. Choosing them is a substantive decision, not a technicality. We pick ten variables about who lives in the household and how. Each one is something a programme designer would care about, and none of them is income:

    • the head of household: age, years of schooling, weekly hours worked (0 if not working), female head
    • the household: size, children under 18, people over 60, rural, owns the home, overcrowded

    Categorical columns become 0/1 with plain pandas comparisons. One column needs care: head_weekly_hours is missing both for heads who do not work (they get 0) and for 87 working heads who did not know their hours (CASEN codes that as −88 in the raw survey; the course file already turns it into missing). For those 87, 0 would be wrong, so we give them the median hours of the people who work.

    In [2]:
    Copied! 
    def weekly_hours(df):
    """Hours worked by the head: 0 if not working; employed but "doesn't know" -> workers' median."""
    h = df["head_weekly_hours"]
    h = h.fillna(h.median()).where(df["head_activity"] == "employed", 0)
    return h

    2. Standardise first¶

    Both PCA and k-means work with distances and variances. Age has a standard deviation of about 16 years, while has_children has one of about 0.5. Without scaling, age and hours would decide everything. StandardScaler puts every column at mean 0 and standard deviation 1.

    There is no train/test split today. We are describing the data, not predicting new households, so there is no test error to protect. Section 9 comes back to what "validation" can mean here.

    3. PCA with two variables: see the idea¶

    Take just age and schooling. Older heads of household in Chile had less schooling, so the two are negatively correlated. PCA rotates the axes. PC1 is the direction along which the cloud is most spread out, and PC2 is perpendicular to it.

    PC1 weights age and schooling equally, with opposite signs: it measures "older and less schooled" versus "younger and more schooled". With two standardised variables, PC1 always captures $(1 + |r|)/2$ of the variance: here $(1 + 0.498)/2 = 0.749$.

    5. k-means, by hand first¶

    k-means looks for k groups whose members are close to their group's centre (the centroid). It alternates two steps until nothing changes:

    1. Assign each household to its nearest centroid.
    2. Update each centroid to the mean of its households.

    Here is the six-household example from the session page, checked with numpy:

    6. k-means on the households, and how to choose k¶

    Inertia is the total squared distance from each household to its centroid. It always falls as k grows: with k = 15,000, every household is its own cluster and inertia is 0. So we look for an elbow, the point where adding clusters stops helping much.

    The silhouette compares, for each household, the distance to its own cluster with the distance to the nearest other cluster. It runs from −1 to 1, and higher means better-separated clusters. It is slow on 15,000 rows, so we compute it on a random 3,000.

    Local minima: why n_init matters¶

    k-means starts from random centroids (chosen with the k-means++ rule) and only ever moves "downhill". So it can get stuck in a solution that is good locally but not the best one. Watch k = 4 started from five different seeds, one run each:

    Different starts, different inertias, different clusters. The fix is cheap: n_init=50 runs the algorithm 50 times and keeps the run with the lowest inertia. With fewer runs (for example 10) you can still land in a worse solution on this data, so always report n_init and random_state.

    An honest reading: there is no sharp elbow, and every silhouette is low (0.21 to 0.25). Households form a continuum, not well-separated islands. That is normal with social data, and it is worth saying out loud.

    The two criteria do not agree perfectly. The silhouette is highest at k = 3. But k = 3 puts families with and without children in one group (57% with children), which is exactly the distinction a family programme cares about. Going from 3 to 4 clusters still cuts inertia by 12,100, while from 4 to 5 the gain drops to 8,300. We take k = 4, and we say why. Choosing k is a judgement call, and you should defend it with numbers and with the use you will give the segments.

    10. Hierarchical clustering: a second opinion¶

    Agglomerative clustering starts with every household on its own and repeatedly merges the two closest groups. With Ward linkage, "closest" means the merge that increases within-cluster variance the least, the same objective as k-means. The dendrogram shows the whole merge history. Cutting it at a height gives clusters. It needs all pairwise distances, so we use 500 households.

    In [19]:
    Copied! 
    from scipy.cluster.hierarchy import linkage, dendrogram, fcluster
