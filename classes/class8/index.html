    Session 8 · Unlabeled Data: What Types of Households Are There?

    Friday 20 November 2026 13:00 – 16:00 Syllabus block F · Unlabeled data Checkpoint 1 feedback · project clinic
    Notebook Open in Colab Check your understanding

    Two halves today

    The first two hours cover PCA and clustering. The last 50 minutes are the project clinic: you get your Checkpoint 1 feedback and work on the final delivery with the instructor going group by group. Bring your notebook.

    ❓ Today's question

    Social programmes are designed for profiles: older people living alone, young families with children, overcrowded households. Each needs a different offer. What types of households are there, if we never tell the algorithm who is poor? And once we have them, how different is poverty from one type to another?

    🔁 What you already know

    Everything so far was supervised: every model learned from an answer, either income (Sessions 3–4) or the label poor (Sessions 5–7). From Session 4 you know why distances need StandardScaler. From Session 2 you know groupby, which is all you need to describe a group.

    ✨ New today

    📖 Contents

    1. No target, only features

    In supervised learning, the error tells you whether you are right. In unsupervised learning there is no answer to check against, so the questions change:

    Supervised (Sessions 3–7) Unsupervised (today)
    "How well do we predict poor?" "Which households resemble each other?"
    Test error decides No test error. Usefulness and stability decide
    The target guides the model The variables you choose guide the model

    That last row is the key to today. We describe a household with ten variables about who lives in it and how: the head's age, schooling and weekly hours (0 if not working), female head, household size, children under 18, people over 60, rural, owns the home, overcrowded. Income and poor are never inputs. Poverty only enters at the end, to describe the groups.

    One column, two meanings of missing

    head_weekly_hours is missing for heads who do not work (those get 0) and for 87 working heads who did not know their hours. In the raw survey CASEN codes that "doesn't know" as −88, a trap the course file already removes. For those 87 heads, 0 would be wrong, so the notebook gives them the median hours of the people who work.

    2. PCA: the direction of most spread

    Picture the cloud of households on two axes, age and schooling (both standardised). Older heads had less schooling, so the cloud leans: it is long in one direction and narrow in the other. PCA rotates the axes so that the first one, PC1, runs along the long side, and PC2 is perpendicular to it. Nothing about the households changes. We only describe them in better coordinates.

    Formally, PC1 is the unit vector \(w\) that maximises the variance of the projections \(z_i = w^\top x_i\). In plain words: among all ways of reducing each household to one number, PC1 keeps the most of how households differ. Each component has:

    • loadings (components_): how much each original variable contributes;
    • explained variance ratio: the share of total variance it captures;
    • scores: each household's coordinate on it.

    With all ten variables, PC1 explains 31% of the variance and PC1 + PC2 together 45%:

    Variable PC1 loading PC2 loading
    head_age 0.50 0.13
    has_elderly 0.46 0.15
    owns_home 0.24 0.20
    household_size −0.26 0.60
    has_children −0.37 0.48
    overcrowded −0.16 0.36
    head_schooling_years −0.34 −0.31
    head_weekly_hours −0.35 −0.23

    PC1 ≈ life stage: older, retired homeowners versus working heads with children. PC2 ≈ size and crowding. You need six components to pass 80%: household life does not collapse into a single index.

    From econometrics to ML

    • PCA is close to factor analysis and to the indices you have seen built from survey variables. The DHS wealth index is the first principal component of household assets (Filmer & Pritchett, 2001), and PCA-based welfare indices are themselves used for targeting.
    • PCs are uncorrelated by construction. Using the first few as regressors is principal components regression (ISLR §6.3), a remedy for multicollinearity.
    • A component has no causal meaning. "Life stage" is a name we give to a direction.
    • Clustering has no ground truth: unlike a regression, there is no residual or test error that says you got it right. You judge clusters by stability and by usefulness.

    3. k-means: assign, update, repeat

    k-means looks for k groups whose members are close to their group's centre, the centroid. It alternates two steps until nobody switches:

    1. Assign each household to its nearest centroid.
    2. Update each centroid to the mean of its households.

    Each step can only lower the inertia, the total squared distance from households to their centroids: \(\sum_i \lVert x_i - \mu_{c(i)} \rVert^2\). Press Step and watch both moves on real households, drawn on the first two principal components:

    Worked example by hand

    Six households, two standardised variables: age of head (\(z_\text{age}\), positive = older) and household size (\(z_\text{size}\), positive = larger). k = 2, starting centroids at households A and D.

    A B C D E F
    \(z_\text{age}\) 1.0 1.2 0.8 −1.0 −0.6 0.2
    \(z_\text{size}\) −0.5 −1.0 0.0 1.0 0.4 0.6

    Assign. Squared distance to \(c_1 = (1.0, -0.5)\) and to \(c_2 = (-1.0, 1.0)\):

    • B: \((0.2^2 + 0.5^2) = 0.29\) vs \((2.2^2 + 2.0^2) = 8.84\) → cluster 1
    • C: \((0.2^2 + 0.5^2) = 0.29\) vs \((1.8^2 + 1.0^2) = 4.24\) → cluster 1
    • E: \((1.6^2 + 0.9^2) = 3.37\) vs \((0.4^2 + 0.6^2) = 0.52\) → cluster 2
    • F: \((0.8^2 + 1.1^2) = 1.85\) vs \((1.2^2 + 0.4^2) = 1.60\) → cluster 2 (a close call)

    Update. Cluster 1 = {A, B, C}: \(c_1 = \big(\tfrac{1.0+1.2+0.8}{3}, \tfrac{-0.5-1.0+0.0}{3}\big) = (1.0, -0.5)\). Cluster 2 = {D, E, F}: \(c_2 = \big(\tfrac{-1.0-0.6+0.2}{3}, \tfrac{1.0+0.4+0.6}{3}\big) = (-0.47, 0.67)\).

    Assign again. F is now at \(0.45\) from \(c_2\) and \(1.85\) from \(c_1\), so it stays. Nobody switches: converged. Inertia \(= 0.58 + 0.93 = 1.51\).

    Reading: cluster 1 is older, smaller households; cluster 2 is younger, larger households.

    4. Local minima: why n_init matters

    k-means only ever moves "downhill" from where it starts, so a bad start can leave it stuck. With k = 4 and one run per seed, five seeds give inertias from 87,198 to 95,803 and visibly different clusters. n_init=50 runs the algorithm 50 times and keeps the run with the lowest inertia. On this data, n_init=10 with random_state=42 still ends in a worse solution (90,043), so we use 50. Report n_init and random_state with every clustering result.

    5. Choosing k

    Inertia always falls as k grows: with k = 15,000, every household is its own cluster. So we look for an elbow, where extra clusters stop paying off. The silhouette compares, for each household, the mean distance to its own cluster (\(a\)) with the mean distance to the nearest other cluster (\(b\)): \(s = (b - a) / \max(a, b)\). It runs from −1 to 1, and higher means better separated.

    k Inertia Silhouette
    2 114,527 0.228
    3 99,309 0.251
    4 87,197 0.219
    5 78,908 0.218
    6 73,732 0.217

    An honest reading: there is no sharp elbow, and every silhouette is low. Households form a continuum, not islands. That is normal with social data, and you should say so. The silhouette prefers k = 3, but k = 3 puts families with and without children in one group (57% with children), which is exactly the distinction a family programme cares about. Going from 3 to 4 clusters still cuts inertia by 12,100, while from 4 to 5 the gain drops to 8,300. We take k = 4, and we say why.

    6. Profile first, then look at poverty

    A cluster is a number until you describe it in the original units:

    Older households Families with children Working adults, no children Overcrowded families
    Households 5,888 4,599 3,934 579
    Age of head 70 43 46 42
    Weekly hours worked 9 33 37 31
    With children 8% 100% 0% 89%
    With someone 60+ 97% 13% 13% 20%
    Owns the home 80% 52% 45% 32%
    Overcrowded 0% 0% 0% 100%
    Poverty rate (revealed last) 23.7% 26.4% 15.8% 40.2%

    The algorithm never saw who is poor, yet poverty ranges from 16% to 40% across the clusters (23.1% overall). Two cautions:

    • This is description, not explanation. Clusters do not say why a household is poor.
    • The overcrowded cluster has the highest rate but holds only 233 of 3,464 poor households (7%). A subsidy aimed at that cluster alone would miss 93% of the poor. Targeting is the job of the supervised models from Sessions 5–7. Clusters help design what to offer each group.

    7. Hierarchical clustering: a second opinion

    Agglomerative clustering starts with every household on its own and repeatedly merges the two closest groups. With Ward linkage, "closest" means the merge that increases within-cluster variance the least, the same objective as k-means. The dendrogram shows the whole merge history, and cutting it at a height gives clusters. It needs every pairwise distance, so the notebook uses 500 households. Cut into four groups, 437 of 500 (87%) fall into clusters that match the k-means ones: two algorithms, same four profiles. The disagreements are households that sit between profiles.

    Common mistakes

    Mistake Why it hurts How to avoid
    Clustering unscaled variables Age (sd 16.5) and hours (sd 22.7) decide everything; unscaled k = 4 just splits by age and hours worked StandardScaler before PCA and k-means
    Putting income or poor in the inputs You "discover" poverty segments you built in Keep the label out; attach it only after clustering
    One run, default seed Local minima: different clusters every time n_init=50, fixed random_state, report both
    Treating a silhouette of 0.2 as "clear types" It signals a continuum, not separate groups Say how weak the structure is; check stability
    Ignoring rare 0/1 variables After scaling, a 1 in overcrowded (4% of households) becomes z ≈ 5 and grabs a cluster Decide deliberately whether that segment is what you want
    Naming clusters as natural or causal types "Life stage causes poverty" is not in the data Names are labels for discussion, not findings

    ✅ Check your understanding

    1. Why must you standardise before PCA? What would PC1 be on the raw variables?

    PCA looks for the direction of largest variance. On raw data, variance depends on units: head_weekly_hours has a standard deviation of about 23 and head_age about 16.5, while the 0/1 variables are below 0.5. PC1 would essentially be "hours and age" because those numbers are bigger, not because they matter more. Standardising gives every variable variance 1, so PCA finds shared patterns rather than large units.

    2. Two standardised variables have correlation r = −0.6. What share of the variance does PC1 keep?

    With two standardised variables, PC1 keeps \((1 + |r|)/2 = (1 + 0.6)/2 = 0.80\), or 80%. The sign does not matter, only the strength. With r = 0 it would keep 50%: no direction is better than another. In our data r = −0.498, so PC1 keeps 74.9%.

    3. Redo the worked example starting from centroids B and F. Where does it end?

    First assignment: A goes to B (0.29 vs 1.85), C goes to F (1.16 vs 0.72), D and E go to F. So cluster 1 = {A, B}, cluster 2 = {C, D, E, F}. Update: \(c_1 = (1.1, -0.75)\), \(c_2 = (-0.15, 0.5)\). Assign again: C is now at 0.65 from \(c_1\) and 1.15 from \(c_2\), so it switches. One more update gives exactly the first solution, {A, B, C} and {D, E, F}, with inertia 1.51. Different starts can take different paths. On real data they can also end in different places, which is why we use n_init.

    4. The silhouette is highest at k = 3. Isn't choosing k = 4 cheating?

    No, as long as you say so. Neither criterion is a test: all silhouettes are low (0.21–0.25), and the elbow is soft. k = 4 separates families with and without children, a distinction with direct policy use, and the inertia gain from 3 to 4 (12,100) is still clearly larger than from 4 to 5 (8,300). Cheating would be reporting k = 4 as "what the data says" and hiding the silhouette.

    5. A minister proposes paying the subsidy only to the 'overcrowded families' cluster, since 40% of them are poor. What do you answer?

    That cluster contains 233 of the 3,464 poor households in the sample: about 7%. Used as a targeting rule, it would have a recall of 0.07, far below the Session 5 logit at any sensible threshold, and it would still pay 346 non-poor households. The clusters are useful to design the offer (overcrowded families may need housing support more than cash). Deciding who gets the subsidy is a prediction problem, and Sessions 5–7 handle it with the right metrics.

    Key takeaways

    • Unsupervised learning has no target and no test error. The variables you choose define the answer, so choose them deliberately and keep the label out.
    • PCA rotates standardised data onto directions of maximum variance. Read the loadings to name a component, and the explained variance to know how much it keeps.
    • k-means alternates assign and update. It finds a local minimum, so run it many times (n_init) with a fixed random_state.
    • Choose k with the elbow, the silhouette and the use you will give the segments, and report all three.
    • Profile clusters in the original units first, and attach the label only afterwards. Clusters describe; they do not target and do not explain.
    Z = StandardScaler().fit_transform(F)                       # F: features, never the label
pca = PCA().fit(Z); pca.explained_variance_ratio_
km = KMeans(n_clusters=4, n_init=50, random_state=42).fit(Z)
silhouette_score(Z[sample], km.labels_[sample])
F.assign(cluster=km.labels_).groupby("cluster").mean()      # profile, then add df["poor"]
