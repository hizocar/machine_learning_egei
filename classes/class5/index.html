    ❓ Today's question

    A social programme does not pay "a predicted income". It decides yes or no. Can we predict directly whether a household is poor, and which mistakes should we be more afraid of?

    🔁 What you already know

    📖 Contents

    1. The same model you know, used to predict

    In econometrics you used the logit to estimate effects. The equation is identical:

    \[P(\text{poor} \mid x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \dots + \beta_p x_p)}}\]

    What changes is the question: not "is \(\beta_1\) significant?" but "how well does this predict households the model has never seen?". Here is the logit with just one variable, years of schooling. Move the coefficients, then let maximum likelihood find them.

    Worked example by hand: from a score to a probability

    The fitted one-variable model is \(z = -0.352 - 0.086 \times \text{schooling}\), and \(p = 1/(1 + e^{-z})\) (coefficients rounded to three decimals, so the notebook may differ in the third decimal).

    Schooling \(z\) \(e^{-z}\) \(p = 1/(1+e^{-z})\)
    8 years −0.352 − 0.688 = −1.040 2.829 1 / 3.829 = 0.261
    12 years −0.352 − 1.032 = −1.384 3.991 1 / 4.991 = 0.200
    16 years −0.352 − 1.376 = −1.728 5.629 1 / 6.629 = 0.151

    Each extra year multiplies the odds of being poor by \(e^{-0.086} = 0.918\), so four more years multiply them by 0.709. Notice that no household gets a probability above 0.5 in this model. With a 0.5 threshold, schooling alone would never select anyone.

    From econometrics to ML

    • Same model, different scorecard. In econometrics you ask whether \(\beta_1\) is significantly different from zero. Here you ask how well the model ranks and classifies unseen households.
    • Significant ≠ useful for prediction. With 15,000 rows almost any variable is significant, even when adding it does not move the test AUC. The reverse also happens.
    • Pseudo-R² ↔ AUC and the confusion matrix. They summarise different things: fit of the likelihood vs. ability to separate classes at the thresholds you would actually use.
    • Odds ratios are associations. In a predictive model the coefficients absorb everything correlated with them. Do not read them as causal effects of schooling or region.

    2. The accuracy trap

    About 23% of households are poor, so a "model" that says nobody is poor is already 76.9% accurate. Our logistic regression with all observable variables reaches 79.7%. That looks like a small win, and it hides a disaster:

    predicted not poor predicted poor
    actually not poor 2,754 130
    actually poor 633 233

    At the default threshold of 0.5 the model misses almost three out of four poor households (recall = 0.27).

    • Recall: of the poor households, the share we reach. TP / (TP + FN)
    • Precision: of the households we select, the share that are actually poor. TP / (TP + FP)

    3. The threshold is a policy decision

    .predict() says "poor" when the probability is above 0.5, but nothing makes 0.5 right. Lower it and the programme reaches more poor households (recall ↑) while subsidising more households that are not poor (precision ↓). How much each mistake costs is not a statistical question. It belongs to whoever runs the programme. Your job is to make the trade-off visible.

    Threshold Poor reached Poor missed Non-poor subsidised Recall Precision
    0.50 233 633 130 0.27 0.64
    0.30 528 338 538 0.61 0.50
    0.20 684 182 1,016 0.79 0.40

    Worked example by hand: what does each threshold cost?

    Say that excluding a poor household costs 3 units and subsidising a non-poor household costs 1. Using the table above:

    • Threshold 0.50: 3 × 633 + 1 × 130 = 2,029
    • Threshold 0.20: 3 × 182 + 1 × 1,016 = 1,562

    Lowering the threshold to 0.20 roughly multiplies the false positives by eight, and the total cost still falls by almost a quarter, because each missed poor household weighs three times as much. The widget finds the cheapest threshold for any pair of costs (≈ 0.24 for 3:1, total 1,482).

    The ROC curve shows every threshold at once, and the AUC summarises it: the probability that a random poor household gets a higher score than a random non-poor one. Ours is 0.79, or 0.78 ± 0.01 with stratified 5-fold cross-validation.

    4. All the information, inside the pipeline

    Most of what we know about a household is categorical, and some values are missing. What missing means decides the rule:

    Column Why it is missing Rule inside the pipeline
    head_weekly_hours the head does not work (6,105 cases) fill with 0, not the median (which would invent a 45-hour week)
    head_weekly_hours an employed head did not know (87 cases) same column, different meaning: 0 is wrong here (notebook exercise 4)
    head_job_type the head does not work its own category, "missing"
    schooling, insurance, overcrowding "doesn't know" (rare) median, or its own category 
    pre = ColumnTransformer([
    ("num",   make_pipeline(SimpleImputer(strategy="median"), StandardScaler()), NUM),
    ("hours", make_pipeline(SimpleImputer(strategy="constant", fill_value=0), StandardScaler()), HOURS),
    ("cat",   make_pipeline(SimpleImputer(strategy="constant", fill_value="missing"),
                            OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore")), CAT),
])
model = Pipeline([("pre", pre), ("clf", LogisticRegression(max_iter=3000))])

    This is the Session 4 pipeline with more steps. Every learned value (medians, categories, scales) is still learned on training folds only.

    5. The leak, again

    Add income_per_capita and the AUC jumps from 0.79 to 0.965. Poverty is defined by comparing income per capita with a poverty line, so this model only rediscovers the definition, and needs the one variable a proxy means test exists to avoid.

    6. Common mistakes

    Mistake Why it hurts How to avoid it
    Reporting accuracy on an imbalanced problem 77% is achievable by selecting nobody Show the confusion matrix, recall and precision next to the baseline
    Leaving the threshold at 0.5 0.5 encodes "both errors cost the same", a policy choice nobody made Choose the threshold from the costs of each error
    Choosing the threshold on the test set The test score becomes optimistic (Leak C again) Use cross-validated predictions on the training set
    Imputing "not applicable" values with the median Invents a 45-hour week for people who do not work Ask what missing means; use 0 or a "missing" category
    One-hot encoding before the split Categories seen only in test leak into training Encode inside the ColumnTransformer with handle_unknown="ignore"
    Splitting without stratify Train and test can end up with different shares of poor households train_test_split(..., stratify=y) and StratifiedKFold

    ✅ Check your understanding

    1. A model has 85% accuracy on a problem where 85% of cases are negative. Is it good?

    You cannot tell from accuracy. "Predict negative for everyone" also scores 85%. Look at the confusion matrix: how many positives does it find (recall), and how many of its positives are right (precision)?

    2. From the matrix at 0.5 (TP = 233, FN = 633, FP = 130, TN = 2,754), compute recall and precision.

    Recall = 233 / (233 + 633) = 233 / 866 = 0.269: the model reaches about one poor household in four. Precision = 233 / (233 + 130) = 233 / 363 = 0.642: of those it selects, about six in ten are poor.

    3. The ministry says: 'we prefer to subsidise some non-poor households rather than miss poor ones'. Do you raise or lower the threshold?

    Lower it. A lower threshold selects more households, so recall rises and precision falls. That is exactly the trade the ministry is willing to make. The cost ratio tells you how far to go.

    4. Our AUC is 0.79. Explain it to a policymaker in one sentence.

    "If you pick one poor and one non-poor household at random, the model gives the poor one a higher score 79% of the time." (A coin would do it 50% of the time.)

    5. Adding income per capita raises the AUC to 0.965. Why should we still not use it?

    Poverty is defined by comparing income per capita with a poverty line, so the model just rediscovers the definition. The point of a proxy means test is that income is not reliably observed when the decision is made. A feature you will not have at prediction time is a leak, however good it makes the score look.

    Assistant skill: choose the metric, don't accept the default

    I have a logistic regression that predicts whether a household is poor (23% positive class).
Here is my confusion matrix at threshold 0.5: (paste). Explain in plain language what each cell
means for a social programme. Then write code that picks the threshold minimising 3*FN + 1*FP on
cross-validated predictions (not on the test set). Tell me why that last condition matters.
    Check that it uses cross_val_predict (or equivalent) on the training set. An assistant that tunes the threshold on the test set has just reintroduced Leak C from Session 4.

    Key takeaways

    • Accuracy can hide a disaster. Always compare with the majority-class baseline and look at the confusion matrix.
    • Recall and precision answer different policy questions: whom do we reach, and whom do we pay by mistake?
    • The threshold is a decision, not a default. It follows from the cost of each error. Choose it with cross-validated predictions.
    • AUC judges the ranking across all thresholds. It is useful for comparing models, not for choosing a cut-off.
    • What missing means decides how to fill it, and every learned step stays inside the pipeline.
    model = Pipeline([("pre", pre), ("clf", LogisticRegression(max_iter=3000))])
p = cross_val_predict(model, X_train, y_train, cv=StratifiedKFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=42),
                      method="predict_proba")[:, 1]
best_t = min(np.linspace(0.05, 0.9, 86),
             key=lambda t: 3 * ((p < t) & (y_train == 1)).sum() + ((p >= t) & (y_train == 0)).sum())
# best_t ≈ 0.26, chosen without ever looking at the test set
