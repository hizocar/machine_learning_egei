    ❓ Today's question

    Our poverty model asks 19 questions in every household visit. Each question costs interview time, can be shaded by an applicant who knows what the programme is looking for, and has to be verified. Which variables do we really need? A form that is half as long and predicts almost as well is a policy win.

    📖 Contents

    1. Why fewer variables?

    More variables never hurt the fit on the training data. They can hurt in three other ways:

    • Variance. Every coefficient is estimated with noise. With few observations per coefficient the model starts fitting that noise, the overfitting of Session 3 in a new form.
    • Interpretability. A model with 7 inputs can be explained to a ministry. A model with 76 columns is a black box even if it is linear.
    • Cost and gaming. In a proxy means test each variable is a question someone must ask, record and verify. The more questions, the more expensive the visit, and the more chances for an applicant to give the answer that qualifies.

    Two families of tools answer the question: selection (keep some variables, drop the rest) and shrinkage (keep all of them, but pull their coefficients towards zero).

    2. Forward stepwise selection: build the form one question at a time

    Trying every possible form would mean \(2^{19} \approx 524{,}000\) models. Forward stepwise selection is the greedy shortcut: start with no questions, and at each step add the one that raises the cross-validated AUC the most.

    We select questions (raw variables), not dummy columns: a form cannot ask "is the region Maule?" without asking the region. Press Play and watch the gains flatten:

    Questions Last question added CV AUC
    1 head_job_type 0.657
    2 head_age 0.702
    3 head_education 0.726
    5 has_children 0.757
    7 region 0.771
    8 head_weekly_hours 0.774
    10 overcrowding 0.779
    19 (all) 0.781

    Eight questions get within 0.01 of the full form (0.774 vs 0.781). Seven reach 0.771, a hair below the line. The 0.01 is a convention, not a law, so show the whole curve and not just the cut-off. The order tells a story too. head_schooling_years only enters at step 18, because head_education already carries almost the same information. Greedy selection keeps one of two correlated variables and ignores the other, which makes the second one redundant, not irrelevant.

    3. Shrinkage: a price on every coefficient

    Selection is all-or-nothing. Shrinkage keeps every variable, but adds a price for large coefficients to the quantity the model minimises:

    \[\underbrace{\text{log-loss}(\beta)}_{\text{fit the data}} \; + \; \lambda \cdot \underbrace{\text{penalty}(\beta)}_{\text{keep coefficients small}}\]
    Method Penalty What it does
    Ridge (L2) \(\sum_j \beta_j^2\) Shrinks all coefficients towards 0. None becomes exactly 0
    LASSO (L1) \(\sum_j \lvert\beta_j\rvert\) Shrinks, and sets some coefficients exactly to 0, so it selects as well
    Elastic Net a mix of both Sparse like LASSO, more stable when variables are correlated

    Read it in plain words: \(\lambda\) is the exchange rate between fit and simplicity. With \(\lambda = 0\) you get the ordinary logit. As \(\lambda\) grows, a coefficient has to "earn" its size by improving the fit enough to pay for it. scikit-learn uses the inverse, \(C = 1/\lambda\), so a small C means a strong penalty.

    Because the penalty compares coefficients with each other, all variables must be on the same scale. A coefficient on income in pesos is tiny and a coefficient on a 0/1 dummy is large, for the same importance. That is why the StandardScaler stays inside the pipeline.

    Worked example by hand

    Take two standardised, uncorrelated variables whose unpenalised (OLS) coefficients are \(\hat\beta_1 = 0.80\) and \(\hat\beta_2 = 0.15\). In this simple case the penalised solutions have closed forms:

    • Ridge divides every coefficient by the same factor: \(\beta^{\text{ridge}} = \hat\beta / (1 + \lambda)\)
    • LASSO subtracts \(\lambda\) and stops at zero: \(\beta^{\text{lasso}} = \text{sign}(\hat\beta)\cdot\max(|\hat\beta| - \lambda,\; 0)\)

    With \(\lambda = 0.2\):

    \(\hat\beta\) (no penalty) Ridge: \(\hat\beta / 1.2\) LASSO: \(\max(\lvert\hat\beta\rvert - 0.2, 0)\)
    \(\beta_1\) 0.80 0.80 / 1.2 = 0.667 0.80 − 0.20 = 0.60
    \(\beta_2\) 0.15 0.15 / 1.2 = 0.125 0.15 − 0.20 < 0 → 0

    Ridge shrinks both coefficients by the same proportion. LASSO shrinks both by the same amount, and the small one does not survive. That is the whole mechanism of selection by LASSO: a coefficient smaller than \(\lambda\) is set to zero.

    Why does the absolute value produce exact zeros and the square does not? A penalty is equivalent to a budget: the coefficients must stay inside a shape (a diamond for L1, a circle for L2), and the solution is the first point where the contours of the loss touch it. Switch between L1 and L2 and shrink the budget:

    The diamond has corners on the axes, and the contours usually touch it at a corner, where one coefficient is exactly zero. The circle has no corners, so coefficients get smaller but never vanish.

    4. The LASSO path on the poverty model

    We fit the L1-penalised logit for 40 values of C, from C = 0.001 (almost every coefficient is zero) to C = 10 (almost no penalty), and record three things each time: the coefficients, the cross-validated AUC, and how many questions still have at least one non-zero coefficient.

    Penalty choice C Coefficients ≠ 0 (of 76) Questions used (of 19) CV AUC (± SE)
    CV-best 0.943 60 18 0.781 ± 0.004
    1-SE rule 0.070 35 18 0.777 ± 0.004

    The 1-SE rule picks the simplest (most penalised) model whose CV AUC is within one standard error of the best, where SE = fold sd / √5. The fold standard deviation is 0.009, so SE = 0.009 / √5 ≈ 0.004, and any model scoring at least 0.781 − 0.004 = 0.777 is statistically indistinguishable from the best. Among those, the rule takes the one with the strongest penalty. It cuts the coefficients from 60 to 35.

    But look at the last column. Those 35 coefficients still come from 18 of the 19 questions. LASSO drops dummy columns, not questions. It may keep region = La Araucanía and drop every other region, and the form still has to ask for the region. To remove questions you need to select whole groups of columns, which is what our forward selection did (the group LASSO does it with a penalty, but it is not in scikit-learn).

    From econometrics to ML

    • OLS is unbiased; that is not the same as accurate. With many correlated regressors its variance is large. Ridge accepts a little bias to remove a lot of variance: the bias–variance trade-off from Session 3, now on coefficients.
    • scikit-learn's LogisticRegression() is already Ridge with C = 1 by default. Stata and statsmodels do not penalise. If you compare coefficients across software, this is why they differ.
    • A LASSO zero is not "not significant". It means "not needed for prediction, given the other variables". Between two correlated variables LASSO may keep one almost at random.
    • Penalised coefficients are biased by design, so the usual standard errors and p-values do not apply to them. If you need inference after selecting variables (for example, the effect of one policy variable controlling for many others), look at post-double-selection (Belloni, Chernozhukov & Hansen, 2014).

    5. Ridge, LASSO, Elastic Net, and the short form, on the test set

    Model Questions asked CV AUC Test AUC
    Full form, logit (Session 5) 19 0.781 0.794
    Ridge, CV-best (C = 0.32) 19 0.781 —
    Elastic Net (0.5), CV-best 19 0.781 —
    LASSO, 1-SE rule 18 0.777 0.795
    Short form, forward selection 8 0.774 0.786

    With 11,250 training households and 76 columns, the unpenalised logit is already stable. All three penalties tie with it on AUC. On this data shrinkage buys sparsity, not accuracy. The real choice is between the full form (0.794) and the 8-question form (0.786), which gives up 0.008 of AUC to save 11 questions per visit. That trade-off belongs to the ministry, but it can now be argued with numbers.

    6. When does shrinkage pay off? When data is scarce

    Imagine a pilot survey in one province: a few hundred households and the same 76 columns. We train on random subsamples of the training set (5 repetitions each) and evaluate on the full test set:

    Training households No penalty Ridge (C by CV) LASSO (C by CV)
    300 0.686 0.733 0.725
    1,000 0.754 0.782 0.779
    11,250 (all) 0.794 0.795 0.795

    With 300 households the unpenalised model has 76 coefficients to estimate from about 70 poor households, and it fits the noise. The penalty cuts that variance and gains almost 0.05 of AUC. With all the data the gap disappears. The fewer observations per variable, the more the penalty is worth.

    Common mistakes

    Mistake Why it hurts How to avoid it
    Penalising unscaled variables The penalty punishes coefficients for their units, not their importance Keep StandardScaler inside the pipeline, before the model
    Reading C as \(\lambda\) You think you are penalising more when you are penalising less Small C = strong penalty. Plot results against C and check
    Selecting variables on the whole dataset, then cross-validating The selection has seen the validation folds, so the CV score is optimistic (a leak) Select inside CV on the training set; test once at the end
    Reading a LASSO zero as "no effect" Correlated variables share the work; LASSO keeps one Say "not needed for prediction given the others"
    Using l1_ratio=1 on an old scikit-learn Versions before 1.8 silently ignore it and fit a Ridge Check sklearn.__version__; count coef_ == 0
    Expecting LASSO to remove questions It removes dummy columns, not whole categorical variables Select raw variables (forward selection) when the unit of cost is the question

    ✅ Check your understanding

    1. Model A uses C = 0.01 and model B uses C = 10. Which one has more zero coefficients, and why?

    Model A. \(C = 1/\lambda\), so C = 0.01 means \(\lambda = 100\), a very strong penalty, while C = 10 means \(\lambda = 0.1\). The stronger the L1 penalty, the more coefficients cannot "pay" for themselves and are set to zero. In the path widget, C = 0.01 is near the left edge, where only a handful of lines have left zero.

    2. A standardised variable has \(\hat\beta = -0.35\). What are the Ridge and LASSO coefficients with \(\lambda = 0.4\)? And with \(\lambda = 0.1\)?

    With \(\lambda = 0.4\): Ridge \(= -0.35 / 1.4 = -0.25\). LASSO: \(|{-0.35}| - 0.4 < 0\), so 0: the variable is dropped.

    With \(\lambda = 0.1\): Ridge \(= -0.35 / 1.1 \approx -0.318\). LASSO: \(\text{sign}(-0.35) \cdot (0.35 - 0.1) = -0.25\). The sign is kept, and LASSO subtracts the same amount whatever the size of the coefficient.

    3. A classmate adds income_per_capita (in pesos) and head_age (in years) to a LASSO without scaling. What goes wrong?

    Two things. First, a leak: income is the variable poverty is defined from (Session 4), so it cannot be a feature at all. Second, even for legitimate variables, units: a coefficient on a variable measured in pesos is tiny (each peso matters little), so the penalty barely touches it, while a variable on a small scale needs a large coefficient and is punished hard. Selection would then depend on the units, not on how informative each variable is. Standardising puts every variable on the same footing.

    4. The 1-SE LASSO has 35 non-zero coefficients but still needs 18 of the 19 questions. Explain, and suggest how to get a shorter form.

    The 19 questions become 76 columns after one-hot encoding, and LASSO penalises each column separately. Keeping a single dummy of a categorical variable (one region, one household type) is enough to force the form to ask that question. To shorten the form, select whole variables: forward selection over raw variables (8 questions for CV AUC 0.774), or a group-LASSO penalty that drops all the dummies of a variable together.

    5. The ministry must choose between the full form (19 questions, test AUC 0.794) and the short form (8 questions, 0.786). Give one argument for each.

    For the short form: 11 fewer questions per visit means cheaper and faster targeting, so more households can be assessed with the same budget. It also leaves fewer answers an applicant can shade, and is easier to explain and audit. For the full form: the AUC gap is small on average, but at the chosen threshold it can mean a few more poor households excluded. Some dropped questions (overcrowding, dwelling type) are observable by the interviewer and hard to fake, which is valuable against manipulation even if their predictive gain is small. A good answer weighs the cost of a visit, the risk of gaming and the cost of each type of error (Session 5).

    Check: did it ask for (or use) your version before answering? Did it give you a check you can run (for example (model[-1].coef_ == 0).sum()), or only a confident explanation?     Key takeaways

    • Every variable has a cost: variance, interpretability, and in policy, collection and gaming.
    • Forward selection adds variables greedily by CV score. Select whole questions when the question is the unit of cost.
    • Ridge shrinks all coefficients, LASSO shrinks and sets some to exactly zero, and Elastic Net sits in between. Always standardise first.
    • In scikit-learn C = 1/λ: small C means a strong penalty. Choose it by CV, and when simplicity has value prefer the 1-SE model: the simplest one whose CV AUC is within one standard error (fold sd / √5) of the best.
    • Shrinkage pays off most when observations are few relative to variables. With plenty of data its gift is sparsity.
