    Session 6 · Trees and Ensembles: Can Flexibility Find Whom the Logit Misses?

    Friday 6 November 2026 13:00 – 16:00 Syllabus block D · Trees and Forests Assistant skill: refactoring into functions
    Notebook Open in Colab Check your understanding

    ❓ Today's question

    The Session 5 logit is a straight line on the log-odds scale: every variable pushes the probability up or down by the same amount, whatever the other variables say. Real poverty is full of interactions: working few hours matters more if there are children to feed. Can a more flexible model find the poor households the logit misses, and what do we give up in exchange?

    🔁 What you already know

    • Session 3: flexible models overfit. KNN with k = 1 memorises.
    • Session 4: pipelines, cross-validation, and never choosing with the test set.
    • Session 5: the logit, AUC, and the threshold as a policy decision (cost = 3·FN + FP), chosen with cross_val_predict on the training set.

    ✨ New today

    📖 Contents

    1. A tree is a sequence of questions

    Imagine a social worker with a clipboard. The first question splits the households into two piles; each pile gets its own next question, and so on. After a few questions, each final pile (a leaf) is a group of similar households, and the model predicts the share of poor households in that pile.

    The tree learns which questions to ask. To do that it needs a way to say how good a pile is.

    2. Choosing a question: the Gini impurity

    A group with a share \(p\) of poor households has Gini impurity

    \[G = 1 - p^2 - (1-p)^2 = 2p(1-p)\]

    In words: pick two households at random from the group. \(G\) is the probability that one is poor and the other is not. A pure group (all poor, or none) has \(G = 0\); a 50/50 group has the maximum, \(G = 0.5\). A split is scored by the weighted average impurity of the two groups it creates, and the tree keeps the split with the lowest score.

    Worked example by hand

    Eight households, three of them poor:

    Household Years of schooling Household size Poor
    H1 6 5 ✔
    H2 8 3 ✔
    H3 8 2
    H4 10 6 ✔
    H5 12 3
    H6 12 1
    H7 14 4
    H8 16 2

    Before any split: \(p = 3/8 = 0.375\), so \(G = 2 \times 0.375 \times 0.625 = 0.469\).

    Split A, "schooling ≤ 10?" - Yes group: H1–H4, 3 poor out of 4, so \(G = 2 \times 0.75 \times 0.25 = 0.375\). - No group: H5–H8, 0 poor out of 4, so \(G = 0\). - Weighted: \(\tfrac{4}{8} \times 0.375 + \tfrac{4}{8} \times 0 = 0.188\).

    Split B, "household size ≤ 3?" - Yes group: H2, H3, H5, H6, H8, 1 poor out of 5, so \(G = 2 \times 0.2 \times 0.8 = 0.320\). - No group: H1, H4, H7, 2 poor out of 3, so \(G = 2 \times \tfrac{2}{3} \times \tfrac{1}{3} = 0.444\). - Weighted: \(\tfrac{5}{8} \times 0.320 + \tfrac{3}{8} \times 0.444 = 0.367\).

    Split A removes \(0.469 - 0.188 = 0.281\) of impurity and split B only \(0.102\), so the tree asks about schooling first. A real tree does this for every variable and every possible cut, then repeats inside each group.

    Now try it on the 11,250 training households. On real data no single variable separates poor from non-poor cleanly. The best cut on schooling (≤ 12) only takes the impurity from 0.355 to 0.346.

    Notice what the tree does not need: scaling. "Schooling ≤ 12" is the same question in years or in standard deviations. Categories still need one-hot encoding, because scikit-learn's trees split on numbers.

    3. The tree we actually get

    Fitted on all the variables, the first question is "does the head work 27.5 hours a week or less?", a group that includes heads who do not work at all. On the yes side 33% of households are poor; on the no side, 13%. The next questions differ by branch: for low-hours households the tree asks about the head's age, then about housing tenure or household size; for full-time workers it asks whether there are children, then about the type of job or schooling. That is an interaction, found without anyone writing it down.

    4. How deep? Overfitting, again

    Every extra level doubles the number of groups. Deep enough, each leaf holds a handful of households and the tree memorises them: it is KNN with k = 1 all over again. Move the slider and watch both the curves and the tree.

    Tree Train AUC CV AUC
    max_depth=3 0.738 0.735
    max_depth=6 (best by CV) 0.795 0.759
    max_depth=12 0.929 0.693
    No limit 1.000 0.618
    No depth limit, min_samples_leaf=100 — 0.763
    Logit, for reference 0.789 0.781

    Even the best single tree stays below the logit. A tree is flexible but unstable: change a few households and the first question can change, and everything below it with it.

    5. Many trees: bagging and random forests

    The cure for instability is to grow many trees and average them.

    1. Bootstrap: draw 11,250 households with replacement from the training set. Some appear twice, and about a third are left out.
    2. Bagging (bootstrap aggregating): fit one deep tree per bootstrap sample and average their probabilities. Each tree overfits in its own way, and the average cancels much of that noise.
    3. Random forest: at each split, a tree may only consider a random subset of the variables (max_features, by default the square root of their number). The trees become more different from each other, so their average improves further.

    The households a tree never saw are its out-of-bag sample: a free validation set.

    Model CV AUC
    Bagging (all variables at each split) 0.787 ± 0.004
    Random forest (300 trees, min_samples_leaf=5) 0.797 ± 0.007
    Random forest, out-of-bag AUC (no CV needed) 0.798

    6. Trees that learn from their mistakes: gradient boosting

    A forest grows its trees independently and averages them. Boosting grows them in sequence: each new tree is small, and it is fitted to what the current model still gets wrong. It is added with a small weight (learning_rate), so the model improves step by step. Too many steps overfit, so HistGradientBoostingClassifier sets aside 10% of the training data and stops early when that held-out score stops improving.

    From econometrics to ML

    • In a regression, an interaction exists only if you write hours × children into the model. A tree finds interactions and thresholds on its own. That is its strength, and also why it overfits.
    • A tree has no coefficients and no standard errors. You cannot ask "is schooling significant?". You can ask "how much worse does the model predict without schooling?" (section 8).
    • Bagging is the bootstrap you know from econometrics. There it estimated the variability of an estimator; here it reduces the variability of a prediction by averaging.
    • None of these models is causal. A tree that splits on hours of work is not saying that more hours would lift a household out of poverty.

    7. The race, done properly

    Four models, compared with the same 5 folds and the same test set. Each model gets its own threshold, chosen to minimise 3·FN + FP on out-of-fold probabilities (cross_val_predict) from the training set, never on the test set.

    Model Train AUC CV AUC Test AUC Threshold Recall Precision Cost 3·FN + FP
    Logit (Session 5) 0.789 0.781 ± 0.009 0.794 0.26 0.680 0.454 1,540
    Tree (depth 4) 0.757 0.746 ± 0.009 0.767 0.24 0.682 0.446 1,560
    Random forest 0.903 0.797 ± 0.007 0.814 0.24 0.745 0.460 1,421
    Gradient boosting 0.910 0.798 ± 0.007 0.815 0.20 0.788 0.439 1,422

    The ensembles gain about 0.02 of AUC and cut the policy cost by about 8%. The forest reaches 75% of poor households against the logit's 68%, at slightly better precision; boosting picks a lower threshold and reaches 79%, at lower precision, for the same total cost. That gain is real, but it is not a revolution, and it comes at a price (section 9).

    8. What does the forest rely on? Permutation importance

    feature_importances_ (impurity importance) adds up how much each column reduced the Gini while the trees were being trained, and it favours variables with many possible cut points. Permutation importance asks a cleaner question on the test set: if I shuffle this column so it carries no information, how much AUC does the model lose?

    Variable AUC lost when shuffled
    head_age 0.037
    head_weekly_hours 0.031
    has_children 0.029
    housing_tenure 0.028
    household_type 0.019
    has_elderly 0.018

    Two cautions. Importance describes what the model uses to predict, not what causes poverty. And when two variables carry the same information (years of schooling and education level), shuffling one costs little, because the other covers for it.

    9. Accuracy vs. explanation

    A household excluded from a programme has the right to ask why. With a depth-3 or depth-4 tree the answer is a short chain of questions: "the head works more than 27.5 hours, there are no children, and the head is not self-employed". With 300 trees there is no short answer. Against the depth-4 tree, the forest gains about 0.05 of AUC and saves about 140 cost units on 3,750 test households. Whether that is worth the loss of transparency is a decision for the programme. Your job is to put both numbers on the table.

    Common mistakes

    Mistake Why it hurts How to avoid it
    Growing a tree with no limits Train AUC 1.000, CV AUC 0.618: pure memorisation Set max_depth or min_samples_leaf and choose them by CV
    Comparing models on different splits Differences can be luck of the split, not the model Same cv object and same test set for every model
    Reading feature_importances_ as causal effects Importance is about prediction, and impurity importance is biased Use permutation importance, and still don't read it causally
    Choosing each model's threshold on the test set The comparison becomes optimistic, the Leak C of Session 4 cross_val_predict on the training set
    Keeping the forest because its AUC is highest You lose the ability to explain each decision Report the trade-off and let the decision-maker weigh it
    Scaling features "just in case" before a tree Harmless but useless, and it adds a step you must explain Trees only need numbers, not comparable scales

    ✅ Check your understanding

    1. A leaf holds 50 households, 10 of them poor. What is its Gini impurity, and what probability does the tree predict for a new household that lands there?

    \(p = 10/50 = 0.2\), so \(G = 2 \times 0.2 \times 0.8 = 0.32\). The tree predicts the leaf's poverty share: a probability of 0.2. Whether that household gets the subsidy depends on the threshold (0.24 for the forest, 0.26 for the logit), not on the tree.

    2. The unrestricted tree has a train AUC of 1.000 and a CV AUC of 0.618. Explain both numbers.

    With no depth limit, the tree keeps splitting until each leaf is pure, often a single household, so it predicts every training household perfectly (AUC 1.000). Those leaves describe individual households, not patterns, so on households it has not seen the rules are mostly noise (CV AUC 0.618, much worse than the logit's 0.781). It is the same phenomenon as KNN with k = 1.

    3. Why does a tree not need StandardScaler, while KNN does?

    KNN compares households by distance, and distance depends on units. A tree only asks "is this value above or below a cut?", and the answer is the same in years, months or standard deviations. One-hot encoding is still needed, because scikit-learn's trees only split numeric columns.

    4. Bagging scored 0.787 and the random forest 0.797. What does the forest do differently, and why does it help?

    At each split, each tree of the forest may only look at a random subset of the variables. With bagging, every tree tends to start with the same strong question (hours of work), so the trees are similar and their errors are correlated. Averaging correlated errors cancels little. Forcing the trees to differ makes their errors less correlated, so the average cancels more noise.

    5. The ministry insists that every excluded household can be told why. What do you recommend, and what does it cost?

    A shallow tree (depth 3–4), presented as a scorecard. On our test set the depth-4 tree costs 1,560 against 1,421 for the random forest: about 140 more cost units, mainly poor households left out. One honest option is to use the forest to rank households and the tree to explain the rule, checking that the two rarely disagree, but that is a policy choice, not a statistical one. Put the numbers on the table.

    Key takeaways

    • A tree predicts with yes/no questions chosen to make groups as pure as possible (Gini). It needs no scaling and finds interactions on its own.
    • Unrestricted trees memorise (train AUC 1.000, CV 0.618). Limit depth or leaf size, and choose the limit by CV.
    • Averaging many different trees (bagging, random forests) cancels their noise. Boosting adds small trees that correct each other.
    • On our data the ensembles beat the logit by about 0.02 AUC and about 8% in policy cost: a real but modest gain that costs explainability.
    • Compare models on the same folds, with thresholds chosen out-of-fold, and read importance as predictive, never causal.
